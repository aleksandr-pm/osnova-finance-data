How It's Calculated

One figure throughout: Berkshire's cash, cash equivalents and U.S. Treasury Bills, in billions of dollars â every segment's cash and cash-equivalents line on the consolidated balance sheet plus its Treasury Bill lines. Until 2016 Berkshire held its T-bills inside cash equivalents; from 2016 it reports them on their own line, and they are added back. The history is read from Berkshire's 10-Q and 10-K filings on the SEC's EDGAR, quarter by quarter from Q1 1999 and at each year-end from 1995 to 1998. Before Q3 2002 it is read from the cash-flow statement's consolidated figure, the same total. New quarters are read nightly from Yahoo Finance's quarterly balance sheet for BRK-B, whose line Cash, Cash Equivalents And Short Term Investments equals the filings on every quarter both carry.

What is the BRKA Cash?

Berkshire Hathaway's cash and Treasury bill holdings are the money that Warren Buffett â widely considered history's greatest long-term investor â is holding back rather than putting into stocks or acquisitions. A large pile is read as a sign that Buffett cannot find investments at prices he likes. A small one is read as a sign that he has been buying.

Investors watch the pile as an indirect reading of how expensive the market is. Buffett has said many times that he buys only when he finds "wonderful companies at fair prices," so record cash suggests he is not finding those prices. A falling pile means money has gone into purchases, and this site's record shows what the market did after those years. The year-end figure fell eight times between 1996 and 2023, and the S&P 500 was higher a year after the annual report in six of them. In the 20 years the pile rose, the market was higher a year later in 15.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does Buffett hold so much cash instead of investing it?
Buffett has said repeatedly that he puts money to work only when he finds wonderful businesses at fair prices. When he cannot find them â typically because markets are expensive â cash piles up. He also keeps a large cushion as a permanent reserve against catastrophe, which he has said will never fall below $30B.
Is Berkshire's cash a reliable market timing signal?
No. It says something about how expensive Buffett finds the market, not about when the market turns. On this site's record Berkshire's cash never reached $16B through the late 1990s and fell to $4.5B by the end of 1999, so no cash pile came before the dot-com crash. The pile then built through 2013â2019, from $48B to $128B, while the S&P 500 rose 75%.
What does Berkshire invest its cash in?
The vast majority of Berkshire's cash is held in short-term US Treasury bills â Buffett has repeatedly said that Treasury bills are the safest place for very large sums. At current interest rates (3-5%) the cash earns a meaningful return, so holding it costs much less than it did when rates were near zero.
When has Buffett deployed cash most aggressively?
Most famously during the 2008 financial crisis â the Goldman Sachs, GE and Burlington Northern deals. He also bought heavily in the 1970s market slump and the early 1990s recession, and selectively in the 2020 COVID crash (Japanese trading companies and Apple shares). Each time, he put cash to work when fear was at its height.
Does Berkshire's cash level affect Berkshire stock (BRK.A, BRK.B)?
A big cash pile gives Berkshire choices: it can make large acquisitions when the right opportunity comes along, which some investors see as a plus for the stock. Buffett has also used spare cash to buy back Berkshire shares when he judged them undervalued.
